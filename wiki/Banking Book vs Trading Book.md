# Banking Book vs Trading Book

> [!note] Core Distinction
> The **trading book** contains positions held with short-term [[Trading Intent]] — for resale, market-making, or hedging other trading positions. The **banking book** holds positions for long-term purposes such as loans, investments, and liabilities managed through accrual accounting.

---

## 1. Trading Book

- Positions actively managed for **short-term resale** or benefiting from short-term price differences.
- Subject to **market risk capital requirements**.
- Requires frequent **marking-to-market** or marking-to-model.

## 2. Banking Book

- Positions held to **maturity** or for long-term strategic purposes.
- Exempt from market risk charges but subject to **credit risk** and **operational risk** capital requirements.
- Managed through accrual accounting.

## 3. Boundary Management

The separation between books is a critical regulatory control to prevent **regulatory arbitrage** — moving positions between books to reduce capital charges.

| Aspect | Basel ([[Basel RBC25|RBC25]]) | EBA ([[EBA CRR — Trading and Banking Book|CRR]]) |
|---|---|---|
| Boundary strictness | Stringent — tight supervisory controls (RBC 25.7–25.10) | More flexible — regional adaptations (Art. 104b(3)) |
| Transfer approval | Requires supervisory approval | Requires documentation + supervisory approval |
| Reclassification | Tightly controlled | Phased approach for EU banks |

> [!warning] Regulatory Arbitrage Risk
> Both frameworks impose strict controls on book transfers precisely because reclassification can materially change a bank's capital requirements.

## 4. Governance

- **Basel**: focuses on clear separation with supervisory oversight but is less prescriptive about internal structures.
- **EBA CRR**: requires granular governance including **trading desk structures**, trading limits, and supervisory reporting (Article 104b).
